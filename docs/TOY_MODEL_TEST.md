# Does a spinor-turned, orthogonality-penalised small transformer carry what it learned on graphs of 10 services to graphs of 100? A toy-model test (written BEFORE the run, 17 Oct 2026)

This page fixes the question, the design, the measures, the predictions and the reading rules before any model of this test has been evaluated on a graph larger than ten services. Code: `python/axi/experiments/toymodel.py`; instrument checks `python/tests/test_toymodel.py`. Concept-map item 103. It needs no GPU, no language model and no internet: numpy on a CPU (a free Colab is enough), about 40 minutes for one seed and about two hours for the three.

## Where the idea comes from, and how it is treated
On 17 Oct 2026 the owner pasted an AI-written roadmap (no author, no sources given; I have not checked with Rajnish Choubey that anything in it is his, so I attribute none of it to him). Its second section, "The 'Toy Model' Tensor Training Benchmark (Proving the Deep Math)", says: "Train two identical small transformers from scratch to predict graph mutations. Arm A (The Industry Standard): Trained with standard 1D Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) and standard dense causal attention. Arm B (The Choubey Architecture): Trained with Lie Group Spinor Embeddings (exp(sum omega^a T_a) in SU(2)) and a loss function enforcing Subspace Orthogonality (|| P_coord W_Q P_payload ||_F^2 -> 0)." What it says this validates: "Zero-Shot Spatial Generalization: Train both models on graphs of size N=10. At inference time, ask them to mutate a graph of size N=100. Arm A will catastrophically fail because 1D RoPE cannot extrapolate spatial coordinates it hasn't seen. Arm B will mathematically succeed because Lie group geodesics are scale-invariant." And: "Elimination of Polysemantic Smear: Measure the principal components of the attention matrices. Arm B will empirically prove that its structural attention heads fire entirely independently of semantic payloads, validating the Grassmannian manifold regularization." It also speaks of "a 10-million parameter 'Toy Model'".

What I can and cannot say. The roadmap gives no task, no definition of the "spinor embedding", no definition of the projectors P_coord and P_payload, and no recipe for either arm. I had to choose all of them, and **every choice below is mine, not Rajnish's**. Four consequences are stated now, before the run, so that they are not read as findings afterwards. (1) "Arm A" as I built it fails at N = 100 almost by construction: its coordinates enter as learned tables of 100 rows per axis, only rows 0 to 9 are ever trained, and the others stay at their random start. That is my way of making "1D RoPE" meet coordinates at all, since a rotation by the index in the sequence carries no coordinate. So lines 5 and 13 are close to true by construction. (2) A stronger and fairer standard baseline exists, the axial 2D rotary embedding (`A_rope2d`, rotation by the coordinates themselves, no tables); it is relative, it depends on the offset between two cells only, and the roadmap does not mention it. (3) The SU(2) turn I built is non-abelian: the attention score of two tokens does not depend on the offset between their cells only. This is tested in the instrument checks, and it is a reason to expect the opposite of the roadmap's sentence on "scale invariance"; I cannot connect that sentence to a property of this construction, and the run measures it instead. (4) The model has about 101,000 parameters (107,000 with the tables), not 10 million.

## The task
A graph is a list of tokens: a query token first, then the services and the edges in a random order. The query holds a cell c. A service token holds its cell (x, y) and a value; an edge token holds the cell of its source, the offset to its target (one of 24: at most 2 away on each axis) and a weight. The model says, for every service and edge token, whether it is hit when the service at c is deleted: a service is hit when it is at c, an edge when its source or its target is at c. (This is my reading of "predict graph mutations"; the query is an existing service in 85 % of the graphs and a free cell next to the graph otherwise.) The cell of a token is its coordinate; the offset, the value and the weight are its payload (ids 1 to 9 in training; ids 10 to 19 exist only in the payload-shift sets). A graph of N services is grown as a connected blob on an N x N grid with edges between services at most 2 apart, a tree plus about 15 % extra edges. N = 10 has about 23 tokens, N = 100 about 288.
The attention is causal and the query comes first, so **the label of every token depends on the query token and on that token alone**: the task is a matching of coordinates, not a reasoning over the graph. What changes between N = 10 and N = 100 is the range of the coordinates (ten times) and the number of tokens among which the attention must find the query (twelve times). A failure at N = 100 can come from either, and the sets below separate them: `range100_n10` has ten services on a 100 x 100 grid (large coordinates, short sequence), `N20`, `N40` and `N70` are in between.

## The arms
Same model for all: 2 layers, model width 64 (32 numbers for the structure of the token, 32 for its payload), 4 heads of 32 numbers each, of which the first 16 (`pos`) are turned by the position code and the other 16 (`sem`) are not; feed-forward 128; layer norm; a causal softmax attention; one output logit per token; trained from scratch, forward and backward pass written in numpy and checked against finite differences.
| arm | how the coordinate gets into the attention | penalty |
|---|---|---|
| `A_rope1d` | **the roadmap's Arm A.** The `pos` part is turned by the index of the token in the sequence (standard rotary embedding); the cell enters only as two learned tables (100 rows each) added to the token | none |
| `A_rope2d` | a stronger standard baseline: the `pos` part is turned by the cell itself, 4 frequencies for x and 4 for y (axial rotary embedding); no tables | none |
| `B_su2` | **the roadmap's Arm B, my reading.** The `pos` part is cut into 4 blocks of two complex numbers per head; block b is multiplied by the SU(2) element exp(theta_b (x T1 + y T2)) with T_a = -i sigma_a / 2 (the Pauli matrices), theta = 1, 0.4, 0.16, 0.064; no tables | lambda = 0.05 |
| `B_su2_free` | the same turn without the penalty (an ablation of mine) | none |
| `A_rope2d_orth` | the axial rotary embedding with the penalty (an ablation of mine) | lambda = 0.05 |
The penalty is my reading of ||P_coord W_Q P_payload||_F^2 -> 0: lambda times the sum of the squares of two blocks of every query and key matrix of every layer, the weights that carry the payload part of the input into the `pos` columns and the weights that carry the structural part of the input into the `sem` columns. It is added to the loss. Half of all query and key weights are in these blocks.

## Training and evaluation
4,000 steps of 32 graphs of 10 services (fresh graphs every step, seeded), Adam, learning rate 0.002 falling by a cosine to 0.0002, gradient norm clipped at 1, a positive token weighs 3 in the cross entropy; three seeds (1, 2, 3) differing in the initial weights and the training graphs. The evaluation graphs are the same for every arm and seed (a digest of them is printed): N10 400 graphs, N20 400, N40 300, N70 200, N100 200, `range100_n10` 400, `pay_shift_N10` 400 (ten services, payload ids 10 to 19 never seen in training), `pay_shift_N100` 200. The measure is **exact**: the share of graphs in which every service and edge token is right (the all-zero answer is reported next to it). The F1 of the positive tokens, the share of graphs with a false alarm and with a miss are reported too. Two further numbers per trained model: the **off-block share**, the share of the squared weight of the query and key matrices that sits in the penalised blocks, and the **payload sensitivity**, the mean total-variation distance between the attention rows of layer 1 for the same graphs (100 graphs of N10) when their payload ids are resampled.
What the penalty does to the training is its own story: the penalty arms see the same graphs and steps as the others.

## What I looked at before locking this page (and what I did not)
I looked at: the gradient of every parameter of every arm against finite differences (all agree to 1e-5), the closed form of the SU(2) element against the matrix exponential, that every turn is orthogonal and its inverse is its transpose, that the rotary embedding on the cell depends on the offset only and the SU(2) turn does not, the graphs by eye and by counts (sizes, tokens, edges), and **one development run: seed 1, 2,000 steps, arms `A_rope2d`, `A_rope1d` and `B_su2`, scored on 300 graphs of N10 that are not in any evaluation set** (exact 1.00, 0.54 and 0.94; F1 1.00, 0.89 and 0.99). Because `A_rope1d` was still learning at 2,000 steps (its loss was falling), I changed the training length from 3,000 to 4,000 steps for every arm; I changed nothing else in the light of it. I did **not** evaluate any model on a graph of more than ten services or on a payload-shift set, the evaluation sets have not been scored by anything, and I have not looked at any attention matrix.

## Predictions (mine, written first)
Each line carries my probability that it reads "yes". They are the reading rules below, in the same order. A line that comes out "NO" is a finding, not an error. The first four lines are about the instrument; a "NO" there means that the lines which depend on it cannot be read.

1. [guard] every arm in every seed was trained for all its steps with a finite loss, and every evaluation set was scored on all its graphs (96 %)
2. [guard] the evaluation sets hold the sizes they are named for (graphs of 10, 20, 40, 70 and 100 services, with the grid side equal to the size; the range set has 10 services on a grid of 100) (99 %)
3. [instrument] every arm learned the task where it was trained: exact accuracy on N10 at least 0.80, for each of the five arms, mean over the seeds (30 %)
4. [instrument] the all-zero answer is not what the arms learned: on N10 every arm has an F1 of the positive tokens above 0.5 (96 %)
5. [claim] the roadmap's Arm A (rope1d) does not carry what it learned to the large graphs: its exact accuracy on N100 is at most half its exact accuracy on N10 (96 %)
6. [claim] the roadmap's Arm B (su2 with the penalty) keeps what it learned: its exact accuracy on N100 is at least 0.8 of its exact accuracy on N10 (12 %)
7. [claim] Arm B beats Arm A on N100 by at least 0.20 in exact accuracy (18 %)
8. [claim] the stronger standard baseline (rope2d, no penalty) keeps what it learned: its exact accuracy on N100 is at least 0.8 of its exact accuracy on N10 (35 %)
9. [claim] Arm B beats the stronger baseline rope2d on N100 by at least 0.05 in exact accuracy (10 %)
10. [claim] the penalty does what it is built for: the share of the squared weight of W_Q and W_K that sits in the penalised blocks is under 1 % for the arms with the penalty and above 10 % for the arms without (88 %)
11. [claim] the penalty makes the attention less sensitive to the payload: the mean change of the attention rows of the first layer when the payload ids are resampled is smaller for su2 with the penalty than for su2 without (70 %)
12. [claim] the penalty helps when the payload is unfamiliar: on pay_shift_N10 su2 with the penalty has an exact accuracy at least 0.05 above su2 without it (25 %)
13. [claim] range alone is enough to break Arm A: on range100_n10 (10 services, coordinates up to 99) rope1d has an exact accuracy at most half its exact accuracy on N10 (94 %)
14. [claim] size alone is not what breaks the rotation arms: on N40 rope2d and su2 each keep at least 0.8 of their exact accuracy on N10 (15 %)

**Headline** (lines 5, 6 and 7): the roadmap's claim. If all three read "yes", the standard arm loses more than half of what it learned at N = 100, the spinor arm keeps at least four fifths of it, and the spinor arm is better by 0.20. My probabilities are 96 %, 12 % and 18 %; the second and third are strongly tied, and my single number is **10 %**.

Why I think so, line by line. *Lines 1 and 2 (96, 99 %):* the run could diverge in one of fifteen trainings; the sets are generated by the code that the instrument checks cover. *Line 3 (30 %):* the development run had `A_rope1d` at 0.54 at 2,000 steps; the sum of two learned coordinate tables has to be matched against a query and the edge's target is source plus offset, which a table cannot add; I expect it to improve with 4,000 steps but not to be sure of 0.80. The other four arms were at 0.94 or better. A NO here is the finding that "Arm A" does not learn the task at this size and budget, and lines 5, 7 and 13 then compare against a model that was not good where it was trained. *Line 4 (96 %):* the F1 of `A_rope1d` was 0.89 in development. *Line 5 (96 %):* by construction, see above. *Line 6 (12 %):* the SU(2) turn is not translation-equivariant; the model is trained on cells 0 to 9 and asked about cells up to 99, where the angles theta x r / 2 reach values (up to 70 for theta = 1) that no training token showed; and there are twelve times more tokens to find the query among. I expect the score of two tokens to be wrong for large cells. *Line 7 (18 %):* needs line 6 or something near it. *Line 8 (35 %):* the axial rotary embedding depends on the offset only, so it does not see large cells as new; but the offsets between far cells (up to 99 against 9 in training) wrap the high-frequency angles, and the attention has to find the query among twelve times more tokens; I put it a little over a third. *Line 9 (10 %):* B over the stronger baseline needs B to work and the baseline to work less. *Line 10 (88 %):* the penalty drives the blocks down (the development run had 0.00 for `B_su2` at 2,000 steps) and the arms without it stay near their random start (half the weight); it is close to true by construction. *Line 11 (70 %):* the task needs no payload at all, so the model has every reason to give the `sem` columns of the penalised arm, which see only the payload, no weight; for the free arm the payload leaks into the scores. I am less sure for the sensitivity than for the weights. *Line 12 (25 %):* with unseen payload ids the free arm may be perturbed more, but both arms may have learned to ignore the payload; the later layers also read the payload from the residual stream, which the penalty does not touch. *Line 13 (94 %):* by construction: the cells of that set are mostly outside 0 to 9. *Line 14 (15 %):* if the SU(2) arm fails at 100 it fails at 40 as well, and the axial arm may fail there too.

What each outcome would mean, written now so that I do not read it afterwards to suit the numbers. *Lines 5, 6 and 7 all yes:* the roadmap's claim holds in its fair form in this toy, with the caveat that Arm A fails by construction; the first things to check would be the non-equivariance test and the evaluation sets, and the second the stronger baseline: if line 8 is also yes, B is not needed. *Line 5 yes, line 6 NO:* what the roadmap says about "scale invariance" of the Lie-group turn does not hold for this turn: the failure at 100 comes from the construction (a non-abelian turn depends on absolute cells), and the test cannot tell whether a different SU(2) code would do better. *Line 8 yes and line 6 NO:* the relative code does what the roadmap attributes to the spinor, and a simpler one does it. *Line 3 NO:* "Arm A" does not learn the task here; nothing about it at N = 100 is a finding. *Line 10 NO:* the penalty is not driving the blocks to zero at lambda = 0.05 in 4,000 steps; the two lines after it mean little. *Line 11 yes:* the penalty reduces how much the first attention layer looks at the payload, which is the narrow thing the roadmap calls "structural heads that fire independently of payloads" (the test does not touch principal components). *Line 12 yes:* the penalty helps under a payload shift; small, and needs a second seed.

## Reading rules (fixed now; the printout prints exactly these)
The fourteen lines above are the reading rules; `toymodel.verdict_toymodel` computes them from the saved result. Every figure is the mean over the three seeds. Thresholds, as in the code: line 3: 0.80; line 4: 0.5 (strictly above); line 5: half; lines 6, 8, 14: four fifths; line 7: 0.20; line 9: 0.05; line 10: 1 % and 10 %; line 12: 0.05. Line 11 is a strict "smaller". Line 14 needs both `A_rope2d` and `B_su2`. A rule about an arm the run does not hold reads "not run"; the real run holds all five.

## What this cannot show
A model of about 100,000 parameters, one synthetic task that is a matching of coordinates, one grid geometry, three seeds, a training length of 4,000 steps. The roadmap's recipe is not given: the SU(2) code (which generators, which frequencies, applied to the cell and not to anything else), the projectors of the penalty and its weight are my choices, and a different reading could behave differently. Nothing about 10 million parameters, language, a real graph-editing task, the "Grassmannian manifold", principal components of attention matrices (the two proxies, the off-block share and the payload sensitivity, are not that), or whether a model trained at larger scale would behave alike. The attention in layer 2 is not measured. The step from 10 to 100 services is a change of range and of length together, and the arms differ in how they are exposed to each. The training uses graphs of one size only; training on a mix of sizes would make the question moot.

## Related work (from memory, not searched; check before citing)
Rotary position embeddings (Su et al., 2021); the length-generalisation problem of position codes (Press et al., 2021, "Train short, test long" with ALiBi, among others); axial and 2D rotary embeddings for images (for example Heo et al., 2024, "Rotary position embedding for vision transformer"); rotary embeddings generalised to Lie groups and learned skew-symmetric generators (Ostmeier et al., 2024, "LieRE", if I remember its name right); soft orthogonality regularisers for weight matrices. If these hold as I remember them, relative position codes are the standard way to get a position code that carries over to larger ranges, which is why `A_rope2d` is in the table, and the Lie-group version already exists in the literature in a form that is built for translation-equivariance; the SU(2) turn with exponential coordinates here is not, and that is the part of this test that I expect to matter.

## How it is run
In `python/`, one seed at a time (so that the three seeds can run on three Colab tabs or accounts): `python -m axi.experiments.toymodel --seeds 1 --out axi_toymodel_seed1.json`, the same with seeds 2 and 3, then `python -m axi.experiments.toymodel --merge axi_toymodel_seed1.json axi_toymodel_seed2.json axi_toymodel_seed3.json --out axi_toymodel.json`. One seed is five trainings of 4,000 steps; I measured 210 to 280 seconds for 2,000 steps of a single arm while other jobs shared the two CPUs of my workspace, so one seed should take about 40 minutes on two CPUs (an estimate, not a measurement). `--quick` runs a few seconds of a tiny sample, to see that it works, not to read. The notebook `python/colab/axi_toy_model.ipynb` does it from a zip with copy-paste cells. ## What I read from it (derived afterwards; none of this was pre-registered)
1. **The roadmap's comparison, as I built it, has one half that holds and one that does not.** Arm A fails at N100 (0.8 %; per seed 0.0 / 2.5 / 0.0), as the page said it would, by construction. Arm B fails there too (2.3 %; per seed 1.5 / 4.0 / 1.5), at about the same level as Arm A and far below the stronger baseline that the roadmap does not mention, `A_rope2d`, which keeps 76.8 %. In this toy the only arm that carries a good part of what it learned to 100 services is the one whose position code depends on the offset between two cells (76.8 %, with a wide spread between seeds); the spinor arm as I built it does not.
2. **Where the SU(2) arm breaks.** It already loses about half at N20 (51.5 % against 99.6 % for `A_rope2d`), is at 14.8 % at N40 (`A_rope2d` 98.2 %) and at 5.5 % at N70 (`A_rope2d` 90.2 %). On `range100_n10`, ten services with coordinates up to 99, it gets 20.7 % while `A_rope2d` gets 100.0 % and `A_rope1d` 11.2 %. So the range of the coordinates alone breaks it, which is what the page said a non-abelian turn would do (the score of two cells depends on where they are, not only on how far apart), and its errors at N100 are of both kinds: 96.2 % of the graphs have a false alarm and 64.8 % a miss, F1 0.29; the errors of `A_rope2d` are almost all false alarms (22.7 % of the graphs, against 0.5 % with a miss; F1 0.95). For `A_rope2d` the range costs nothing (100.0 %), so what it loses at N100 comes from the length of the sequence or from the offsets between far cells (wrapping of its fast angles); I did not test those two apart.
3. **The penalty does what it is built for, and does not help the size.** It empties the penalised blocks (0.0002 % of the squared weight for `B_su2`, against 47.5 % without it). At N100 `B_su2` is 2.3 % and `B_su2_free` 2.8 %; at N20 51.5 % against 42.5 %; at N40 14.8 % against 14.9 %. Put on the axial rotary embedding, the penalty lowers N100 in all three seeds: from 76.8 % (61.5 / 89.5 / 79.5) to 58.7 % (41.5 / 67.0 / 67.5), 18.2 points on average (per seed -20.0 / -22.5 / -12.0).
4. **The one place where the penalty looks useful is the unfamiliar payload at the trained size.** On `pay_shift_N10` the penalised axial arm is ahead of the unpenalised one in all three seeds (98.8 % against 90.4 %; per seed +0.8 / +9.3 / +15.3 points), and the penalised SU(2) arm is ahead of its free twin on the mean (+18.3 points) though not in seed 1. On `pay_shift_N100`, where everything is low, the order of the axial pair is on the other side on the mean (33.8 % against 39.0 %) and in two seeds of three. This is a small effect on one set, from three seeds, found by looking; it needs a second look with pre-registered lines before anyone calls it a finding.
5. **"Structural heads that fire independently of the payload" is not what the numbers show.** The penalised arms do have a smaller sensitivity of the first layer to the payload on the mean (0.0672 for `B_su2` and 0.0680 for `A_rope2d_orth`, against 0.0912 for `B_su2_free` and 0.1404 for `A_rope2d`), but it is not zero, it is not smaller in every seed for SU(2), and the lowest value in the table belongs to the roadmap's Arm A (0.0237), which has no penalty. The measure is the first layer only; the second layer and principal components were not measured.

## What this does and does not show about the roadmap's second test
* **"Arm A will catastrophically fail."** As built it does (0.8 % at N100), and the page said that this is nearly true by construction. With a relative position code, which the roadmap does not mention, there is no catastrophe (76.8 %).
* **"Arm B will mathematically succeed because Lie group geodesics are scale-invariant."** This did not hold for the SU(2) turn I built (2.3 % at N100, 51.5 % at N20). It does not show that no spinor or Lie-group code can carry the task: the page said the turn is not translation-equivariant and that the test cannot tell; it also said, from memory and unchecked, that Lie-group rotary codes built to be translation-equivariant exist in the literature. This run says nothing about them.
* **"Its structural attention heads fire entirely independently of semantic payloads."** Only two narrow proxies were measured: the weights in the penalised blocks go to nearly zero (line 10), and the first attention layer moves less with the payload on the mean (line 11), not to zero and not in every seed.
* **Not done and worth doing:** a translation-equivariant Lie-group code in place of my SU(2) turn; more seeds (the baseline's N100 score is 61.5 / 89.5 / 79.5 percent in the three seeds); a mix of sizes in training; 10 million parameters; a real graph-editing task; the second attention layer and principal components; separating range from length for the axial arms. None of these is implied by this result, either way.

## Reproduce
In `python/`, one seed at a time: `python -m axi.experiments.toymodel --seeds 1 --out axi_toymodel_seed1.json`, the same with seeds 2 and 3, then `python -m axi.experiments.toymodel --merge axi_toymodel_seed1.json axi_toymodel_seed2.json axi_toymodel_seed3.json --out axi_toymodel.json`; about 32 minutes per seed on the Colab CPUs. `tests/test_toymodel_result.py` recomputes every figure of this Result from the saved result file.
